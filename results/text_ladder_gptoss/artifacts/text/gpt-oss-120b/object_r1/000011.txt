A teapot is a small, often charming vessel designed to steep tea leaves in hot water and then serve the resulting brew. While designs vary widely across cultures and eras, most teapots share several recognizable features:

### Basic Parts
| Part | Description | Typical Materials |
|------|-------------|--------------------|
| **Body (or vessel)** | The main container that holds water and tea leaves. Usually rounded or slightly oval for even heat distribution. | Ceramic, porcelain, glass, cast iron, stainless steel, clay (e.g., Yixing), or even wood (e.g., Japanese yakimono). |
| **Spout** | A narrow, tapered outlet that directs the liquid into a cup without dribbling. Often angled to control flow. | Same material as the body, sometimes fitted with a thin metal lip (e.g., silver or stainless steel) for durability. |
| **Handle** | An ergonomic grip that stays cool enough to hold while the teapot is hot. Often positioned opposite the spout for balance. | Same material as the body, or a separate material like wood, heat‑resistant plastic, or silicone for insulation. |
| **Lid** | A cover that fits snugly on top, trapping heat and preventing steam from escaping. Often includes a small knob or “handle” for easy removal. | Same material as the body; some lids have a silicone gasket for a tighter seal. |
| **Infuser (optional)** | A removable basket, mesh, or built‑in chamber that holds loose leaves, keeping them separated from the poured tea. | Stainless steel mesh, fine porcelain, or woven metal. |
| **Strainer (optional)** | A peripheral mesh or perforated plate that sits at the spout opening, catching loose leaves as you pour. | Metal (stainless steel) or silicone. |

### Common Shapes & Styles
- **Traditional English:** Often made of pewter or porcelain with a rounded body, a short, flared spout, and a thick, sturdy handle. Decorative patterns such as floral motifs or gilt edges are common.
- **Japanese (Tetsubin or Yixing):** Cast‑iron teapots (tetsubin) are heavy, dark, and develop a patina; Yixing clay pots are porous, allowing the flavor of tea to be absorbed into the walls. Both have modestly curved spouts and a single, sturdy handle.
- **Chinese (Gongfu style):** Small, often teardrop‑shaped gourds (Yixing “gaiwan” teapots) designed for short steep times and multiple infusions. The spout usually curves gently upward.
- **Modern Glass:** Transparent heat‑resistant glass (e.g., borosilicate) lets you watch the leaves unfurl, and the sleek silhouette fits contemporary kitchens.
- **Novelty or Artistic:** Hand‑painted, hand‑thrown ceramics with whimsical faces, animal shapes, or abstract sculptures—these pieces double as décor.

### Functional Characteristics
- **Heat Retention:** Materials like cast iron and thick ceramic store heat well, keeping tea warm for longer. Glass and thin porcelain cool more quickly.
- **Flavor Influence:** Porous clays (e.g., Yixing) can absorb tea oils, subtly altering the taste over time. Non‑reactive materials such as glass, stainless steel, and glazed porcelain impart no flavor.
- **Capacity:** Typically range from 200 ml (for a personal “single‑serve” pot) to 1 L or more for serving a group. A standard household teapot often holds about 500 ml (≈2 cups).
- **Ease of Cleaning:** Removable infusers and wide mouths make cleaning simple. Cast‑iron pots need hand‑washing and should be kept dry to avoid rust, whereas glass and stainless steel are dishwasher‑safe.

### Aesthetic Details
- **Glazing & Color:** Porcelain teapots may be glazed in hues from pure white to deep cobalt blue, often with hand‑painted motifs (e.g., cherry blossoms, dragons, or Art Deco patterns). Matte glazes give a rustic feel, while glossy finishes feel sleek.
- **Texture:** Some teas are intentionally left with a rough texture (e.g., unglazed Yixing) to increase the surface area for flavor infusion.
- **Decorative Accents:** Metal fittings (hinges, knobs) can be brass, silver, or nickel‑plate; some teasers incorporate bamboo or wooden lids for an organic touch.

### Cultural Nuances
- **British Afternoon Tea:** A teapot is central to the ritual, often accompanied by a milk jug, sugar bowl, and a tiered cake stand.
- **Chinese Gongfu Cha:** Teapots are small, allowing rapid infusion and multiple short steeps, emphasizing the tea’s evolving flavor profile.
- **Japanese Tea Ceremony (Chanoyu):** While the ceremony traditionally uses a *kyūsu* (small brewing pot) and *chawan* (tea bowl), teapots in modern Japanese homes may mirror those Western designs, yet often retain minimalist aesthetics.
