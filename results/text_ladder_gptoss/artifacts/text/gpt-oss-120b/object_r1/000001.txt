A teapot is a small, vessel designed specifically for brewing and serving hot tea. Its classic shape and functional elements combine both practicality and aesthetic charm:

**1. Overall Form**  
- **Body:** Usually a rounded, squat or slightly elongated chamber that holds water and tea leaves. The interior volume typically ranges from 300 ml (about 10 oz) for a personal “single‑serve” pot to over 1 liter for a family‑size version.  
- **Spout:** A tapered, curved or straight nozzle extending from the body, allowing a smooth, controlled pour. The spout often includes a small “lip” or “pintle” that helps break the surface tension, preventing drips.  
- **Handle:** An ergonomic grip positioned opposite the spout, often arched or looped, enabling the pot to be lifted and tilted without burning the hand. Handles are sized to accommodate a full pot of liquid comfortably.  
- **Lid:** A fitted cover that seals the top of the pot, usually with a small knob or protrusion for easy removal. The lid may have a small vent or steam escape to regulate pressure while the tea steeps.

**2. Materials**  
- **Ceramic (porcelain, stoneware, earthenware):** Classic choice, offers excellent heat retention, a smooth surface for decorative glazes, and a non‑reactive interior.  
- **Metal (stainless steel, copper, cast iron):** Highly conductive, durable, and often double‑walled for insulation (e.g., “thermal” teapots). Cast‑iron versions are usually enamel‑coated to prevent rust.  
- **Glass:** Transparent, allowing the drinker to watch the tea steep and colors develop. Borosilicate glass resists thermal shock.  
- **Silicone or plastic:** Lightweight, often used for travel or outdoor teapots; may include heat‑resistant silicone lids.

**3. Decorative Features**  
- **Glazes and patterns:** From simple solid colors to intricate hand‑painted motifs (e.g., Chinese “famille rose,” Japanese “raku,” or English “blue willow”).  
- **Embossed or relief designs:** Floral, animal, or geometric motifs raised from the surface.  
- **Engravings or inscriptions:** Names, quotes, or tea‑related symbols.  
- **Finials and decorative knobs:** Small metal or ceramic accents on the lid or spout.

**4. Functional Aspects**  
- **Heat retention:** Thick walls or double‑wall construction keep the brewed tea hot for longer periods.  
- **Steeping capability:** Some teapots include an internal strainer or a removable infuser basket, allowing loose tea leaves to be contained while steeping.  
- **Pour control:** A well‑designed spout minimizes drips and ensures a smooth, steady stream, essential for serving delicate tea ceremonies.  
- **Ease of cleaning:** Smooth interiors (especially in metal or glazed ceramic) prevent tea residue buildup; many are dishwasher‑safe, though hand‑washing is often recommended for delicate glazes.

**5. Cultural Variations**  
- **Chinese Yixing teapots:** Made from porous Yixing clay that absorbs tea oils, enhancing flavor over time. Typically small (80‑150 ml) and without a built‑in strainer.  
- **Japanese Kyūsu:** Small, often with a side handle, designed for brewing a single serving of green tea. Frequently crafted from porcelain or clay.  
- **British “tea service” teapots:** Larger, often ornate, paired with matching cups, saucers, and a sugar bowl, reflecting the tradition of afternoon tea.  
- **Middle Eastern “dallah”** or “samovar” components: Metal vessels used for boiling water and brewing strong tea, sometimes incorporating a central chimney for constant reheating.

**6. Sensory Experience**  
When holding a teapot, the warmth from the hot liquid radiates through the walls, giving a comforting, tactile sensation. The gentle clink of the lid fitting onto the body, the aromatic steam escaping from the spout, and the smooth, controlled pour all contribute to the ritualistic pleasure of preparing and sharing tea.
