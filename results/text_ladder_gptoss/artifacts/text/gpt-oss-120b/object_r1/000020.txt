A teapot is a small, often rounded vessel designed for steeping loose‑leaf tea or tea bags and then serving the brewed liquid. While the exact shape can vary widely across cultures and design styles, most teapots share a few key components:

| Part | Typical Features | Purpose |
|------|------------------|---------|
| **Body (or Vessel)** | A rounded, bulbous chamber that may be slightly wider at the base and taper toward the top. Often made of ceramic, porcelain, glass, metal (stainless steel, cast iron, pewter), or even clay. | Holds the water and tea leaves while they steep, allowing heat to be retained and the infusion to develop evenly. |
| **Lid** | Usually a shallow, slightly domed cover that fits snugly over the opening. Some lids have a small knob or a steam vent. | Keeps heat in, prevents dust from falling in, and can be lifted to release excess steam when the tea is ready. |
| **Spout (or Pouring Lip)** | A narrow, tapered nozzle that may be straight or slightly curved. The interior of the spout is often polished to encourage a smooth, drip‑free pour. | Directs the brewed tea into cups without splashing or spilling. |
| **Handle** | A sturdy, ergonomically shaped protrusion opposite the spout. Handles can be made of the same material as the body, or of insulated materials like wood, silicone, or heat‑resistant plastic. | Allows the teapot to be lifted and poured safely while the body remains hot. |
| **Infuser (optional)** | A removable metal or silicone basket, a built‑in mesh screen, or a separate strainer that holds loose tea leaves. | Keeps the leaves separated from the finished tea, making it easy to serve a clear brew. |
| **Base (optional)** | A flat platform or a raised ring, sometimes with a decorative pattern. For cast‑iron or glass teapots, the base may include a heat‑resistant coaster or stand. | Provides stability on a table and, in some designs, helps distribute heat evenly. |

### Common Materials & Their Characteristics

| Material | Visual / Tactile Qualities | Practical Benefits |
|----------|----------------------------|---------------------|
| **Porcelain / Ceramic** | Smooth, often glazed with colorful, intricate patterns; can be thin‑walled. | Excellent heat retention, easy to clean, non‑reactive (won’t affect flavor). |
| **Cast Iron** | Heavy, matte or enamel‑coated exterior; often with a sturdy, slightly rough feel. | Superior heat retention, durability, can be used on stovetops (if rated). |
| **Glass** | Transparent, sometimes with a subtle tint; smooth surface. | Lets you watch the leaves unfurl; neutral taste; usually dishwasher‑safe. |
| **Stainless Steel** | Brushed or polished metal finish; sleek, modern look. | Highly durable, resistive to breakage, good for travel or outdoor use. |
| **Clay (Yixing, etc.)** | Earthy, porous surface; often unglazed; can develop a patina over time. | Absorbs tea oils, enhancing flavor with repeated use (especially for oolong or pu‑erh). |

### Typical Sizes

- **Miniature (100–150 ml)** – Ideal for a single serving, often used for Japanese “kyūsu” style teapots.
- **Standard (300–500 ml)** – Common for everyday household use; serves 2–4 cups.
- **Large (800 ml–1 L or more)** – Suitable for gatherings or tea ceremonies where many cups are needed.

### Cultural Variations

- **British Teapot**: Traditionally a sturdy ceramic or porcelain piece with a short, stout spout and a wide, comfortable handle; often accompanied by a matching set of cups, saucers, and a milk jug.
- **Japanese Kyūsu**: Typically a smaller, often side‑handle teapot made of porcelain, clay, or glass, with a tightly fitting lid and a low‑profile spout for precise pouring.
- **Chinese Yixing Teapot**: Hand‑crafted from porous zisha clay, usually unglazed, and prized for its ability to absorb and enhance the flavor of certain teas over time.
- **Russian Samovar** (technically a larger kettle rather than a teapot) often features a decorative teapot-shaped top used for serving the brewed tea.

### How It Works (Step‑by‑Step)

1. **Pre‑heat** the teapot with hot water to prevent the temperature from dropping when the tea leaves are added.
2. **Add** the measured amount of loose tea leaves (or a tea bag) to the infuser or directly into the body.
3. **Pour** freshly boiled water (generally 95‑100 °C for black tea, 80‑85 °C for green tea) into the pot.
4. **Steep** for the recommended time (e.g., 3–5 minutes for black tea, 1–3 minutes for green tea). The lid can be left on to retain heat, and a small steam vent may be opened if you want to avoid over‑extraction.
5. **Remove** the infuser or strain the tea into a separate vessel to stop further steeping.
