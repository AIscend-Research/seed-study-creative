A teapot is a small kitchen vessel specially designed for steeping tea leaves or tea bags in hot water, then pouring the brewed tea into cups. Its classic form combines both function and aesthetic appeal, and it typically includes the following parts:

| Part | Description |
|------|--------------|
| **Body (or Vessel)** | The main, rounded or gently tapered container where the tea leaves sit and the water is heated. It’s usually made of ceramic, porcelain, glass, metal (such as cast iron or stainless steel), or even enamel. The interior is smooth to allow easy cleaning and even heat distribution. |
| **Spout** | A tapered outlet that directs the brewed tea into a cup. A well‑designed spout minimizes drips and provides a steady, controlled stream. Some spouts have a short “bite” (a small opening) that helps break surface tension and prevent splashing. |
| **Lid** | A fitting cover that sits snugly on the top of the body. It often has a small knob or handle for easy removal. The lid retains heat and steam, ensuring proper steeping, and sometimes includes a vent to release excess pressure. |
| **Handle** | An ergonomic grip attached to the side of the body, allowing the teapot to be lifted and poured without burning the hand. Handles can be curved, straight, or decorative, and are usually positioned opposite the spout for balance. |
| **Infuser (optional)** | Many modern teapots come with a removable mesh basket or built‑in compartment that holds loose tea leaves while allowing water to flow through. This keeps leaf fragments from escaping into the poured tea. |
| **Base** | The bottom of the teapot, often flat for stability. In some designs—especially those meant for stovetop use—the base may be slightly thicker to distribute heat evenly. |

### Typical Materials & Their Traits

| Material | Pros | Cons |
|----------|------|------|
| **Ceramic / Porcelain** | Keeps heat well, available in many artistic designs, non‑reactive | Can chip if dropped; heavier than glass |
| **Glass** | Shows the tea’s color as it brews; easy to see when steeping is complete | Fragile; doesn’t retain heat as long |
| **Cast Iron** | Excellent heat retention; durable; often comes with enamel interior to prevent rust | Heavy; can be pricey |
| **Stainless Steel** | Lightweight, resistant to rust, often double‑walled for insulation | Can impart a metallic taste if low‑quality; less decorative |
| **Enamel on Metal** | Combines durability with vivid colors; good heat conduction | Enamel can chip; may be less insulated |

### Visual and Aesthetic Elements

- **Shape:** Traditional teapots are round or slightly oval, but contemporary designs may be angular, elongated, or even asymmetrical.
- **Color & Pattern:** From plain white porcelain to intricate hand‑painted motifs, teapots serve as decorative pieces as much as functional tools.
- **Size:** Common capacities range from **150 ml** (a “personal” teapot) to **1 L** (suitable for serving a small group). Larger “family‑size” teapots can hold 2 L or more.
- **Cultural Variations:**  
  - *Japanese (kyūsu):* Often features a side handle and a short, wide spout for precise pouring.  
  - *Chinese (yixing):* Made from porous Yixing clay that absorbs tea flavors over time, enhancing subsequent brews.  
  - *British:* Frequently made of porcelain or bone china, with a high‑arched spout and a decorative lid.

### How It Works

1. **Pre‑heat (optional):** Warm the teapot with hot water to maintain brewing temperature.  
2. **Add Tea:** Place loose leaf tea in the infuser or directly in the body. A typical ratio is about **1 teaspoon (≈2 g) per 200 ml** of water.  
3. **Pour Hot Water:** Use water just off the boil (≈95 °C for most black teas, lower for green or white teas).  
4. **Steep:** Let the tea brew for the recommended time (usually 3–5 minutes for black tea).  
5. **Serve:** Remove the infuser or strain the tea, then pour through the spout into cups.

### Care Tips

- **Cleaning:** Rinse promptly after use. For ceramic or porcelain, mild dish soap is fine; avoid abrasive scrubbers. Metal teapots may need a soft cloth to prevent scratching.  
- **Stain Removal:** Tartaric acid (found in lemon juice) or a mixture of baking soda and water can gently lift tea stains.  
- **Storage:** Keep the lid slightly ajar to prevent odors from lingering, and store the teapot upside‑down or with a breathable cover if it has an infuser.

In summary, a teapot is a versatile, often artistic container that blends form and function to brew and serve tea, embodying centuries of cultural tradition while adapting to modern tastes and materials.
