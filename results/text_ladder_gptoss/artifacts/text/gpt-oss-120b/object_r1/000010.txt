A teapot is a small, often rounded vessel used for brewing and serving hot tea. While designs vary widely across cultures and styles, most teapots share a handful of key elements:

| Part | Description | Typical Materials |
|------|-------------|--------------------|
| **Body (or kettle)** | The main container where water and tea leaves (or a tea bag) combine. Usually a bulbous shape that widens near the bottom and tapers toward the top, allowing ample space for the leaves to unfurl. | Porcelain, ceramic, glass, cast iron, stainless steel, clay (e.g., Yixing), or even enamel-coated metal. |
| **Spout** | A narrow, tapered outlet that directs the brewed tea into a cup without spilling. It often has a slight curve to control flow and may feature a small lip or “bead” that reduces drips. | Same as the body, sometimes stamped or welded on separately. |
| **Handle** | An ergonomically placed protrusion (often opposite the spout) that lets the user lift and pour the teapot safely, even when the contents are hot. Handles can be solid, arched, or ribbed for a better grip. | Same material as the body or, in some metal teapots, a separate wooden or silicone‑capped handle for insulation. |
| **Lid** | A cover that sits snugly on the top opening, sealing in heat and steam while allowing the tea to steep evenly. Many lids have a small knob or crown for easy removal, and sometimes a vent hole to prevent pressure buildup. | Matching material; often slightly domed or flat, with a tight seal. |
| **Infuser (optional)** | A built‑in mesh basket, metal strainer, or removable chamber that holds loose tea leaves, keeping them separate from the liquid when pouring. | Stainless steel, fine‑mesh nylon, or fine‑porous ceramic. |
| **Decorative Elements** | Patterns, glazes, painted scenes, embossing, or metal ornaments that add aesthetic appeal. Classic styles include the English “Blue Willow” pattern, Japanese “Raku” glaze, Chinese “Yixing” clay texture, and minimalist Scandinavian lines. | Varies with artistic tradition. |

### Visual & Functional Overview

- **Shape**: Most teapots are roughly spherical or slightly oval in the body, which provides a generous volume (typically 0.5–1.5 L) while allowing the tea leaves to circulate freely.
- **Balance**: The handle and spout are positioned to keep the teapot stable when lifted; the weight is often centered near the bottom to prevent wobbling.
- **Heat Retention**: Materials like cast iron or thick ceramics retain heat longer, ideal for prolonged steeping. Glass and thin porcelain allow you to watch the infusion process but lose heat more quickly.
- **Cultural Variations**:
  - *Chinese Yixing*: Hand‑pressed clay teapots that absorb tea flavors over time.
  - *Japanese Tetsubin*: Cast‑iron teapots with a lacquered interior, known for their durability and heat retention.
  - *British Teapot*: Often white porcelain with a curved spout and a short, sturdy handle, sometimes featuring a decorative “sugar” or “cream” pitcher set.
  - *Moroccan Mint Teapot*: Small, narrow‑spouted metal teapots (often silver) used for brewing green tea with fresh mint and sugar.

### Practical Tips for Use

1. **Pre‑heat**: Warm the teapot with hot water before adding tea leaves; this helps maintain brewing temperature.
2. **Steeping**: Add loose leaves or a tea bag, pour near‑boiling water (temperature varies by tea type), and let steep for the recommended time—typically 2–5 minutes.
3. **Pouring**: Tilt the teapot gently, using the spout’s lip to control flow. A well‑designed spout prevents drips and provides a smooth, continuous stream.
4. **Cleaning**: Rinse promptly after use. For porous materials like Yixing, avoid detergents to preserve the tea‑flavor “seasoning.” For metal or glass, a mild dish soap and soft sponge work fine.

In essence, a teapot is both a functional kitchen tool and a cherished object of design, marrying practicality with cultural artfulness. Whether you’re brewing a robust English Breakfast, a delicate Japanese Sencha, or a fragrant Moroccan mint infusion, the teapot is the vessel that transforms hot water and leaves into a comforting ritual.
