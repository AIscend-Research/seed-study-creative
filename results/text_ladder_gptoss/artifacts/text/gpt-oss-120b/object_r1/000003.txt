A teapot is a hollow vessel designed specifically for brewing and serving tea. Its classic form combines both function and aesthetic appeal, and it typically includes several key parts:

| Part | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Body (or vessel)** | The main, rounded compartment where loose‑leaf tea or tea bags steep in hot water. It is usually wider at the base and tapers slightly toward the top, allowing the tea to circulate freely. |
| **Spout** | A narrow, often gently curved nozzle that pours the brewed tea smoothly into cups. Many spouts are angled to prevent drips, and the interior may be tapered to guide liquid flow. |
| **Handle** | An ergonomic grip positioned opposite the spout, allowing the teapot to be lifted and poured without burning the hands. Handles can be arced, looped, or sculpted into decorative shapes. |
| **Lid** | A fitted cover that seals the opening at the top of the body, preserving heat and preventing steam from escaping. Lids often have a small knob or handle for easy removal. |
| **Infuser (optional)** | Some teapots contain a built-in metal or mesh basket that holds the tea leaves, keeping them contained while the water steeps. The infuser can be removable for cleaning. |

### Typical Materials

| Material | Characteristics |
|----------|-----------------|
| **Ceramic (porcelain, stoneware, earthenware)** | Retains heat well; can be glazed with intricate designs; often used for traditional English, Chinese, and Japanese teapots. |
| **Glass** | Transparent, showcasing the color of the steeping tea; typically heat‑resistant borosilicate; allows visual monitoring of brew strength. |
| **Metal (stainless steel, cast iron, silver)** | Highly durable and good at heat retention (especially cast iron); may develop a patina over time; often used in Japanese “tetsubin” teapots. |
| **Clay (yixing/zhuni)** | Porous and breathable, absorbing tea flavors over time; prized in Chinese Gongfu tea ceremonies. |
| **Silicone or Plastic (modern, travel‑type)** | Lightweight, break‑resistant, and often insulated; suited for on‑the‑go use. |

### Design Styles

- **Traditional English (Yorkshire, Earl Grey)** – Often a stout, rounded body with a floral or gilt pattern, made from fine china or porcelain.
- **Japanese (Kyusu)** – Usually a side‑handle design (called “kyūsu”) with a slender spout and a thin-walled porcelain or clay body, emphasizing delicate brewing.
- **Chinese (Yixing)** – A sturdy, unglazed purple clay teapot with a short, squat shape; designed for multiple infusions of the same leaf.
- **Modern Minimalist** – Clean lines, matte finishes, often made from glass or stainless steel, focusing on function over ornamentation.
- **Artistic/Novelty** – Shaped like animals, teacups, or whimsical objects; may include decorative motifs such as hand‑painted scenes, embossed patterns, or enamel work.

### How It Works

1. **Pre‑heat** – The teapot is often rinsed with hot water to warm the vessel, helping maintain the temperature of the brew.
2. **Add Tea** – Loose leaves or tea bags are placed directly into the body or into an infuser.
3. **Pour Hot Water** – Boiling (or near‑boiling, depending on the tea type) water is poured over the tea.
4. **Steep** – The tea is left to infuse for a specific time, usually 2–5 minutes for most black or green teas, longer for herbal blends.
5. **Serve** – The lid is removed, and the tea is poured through the spout into cups. The teapot’s shape aids in an even flow, minimizing drips.

### Cultural Touchpoints

- **British “Afternoon Tea”** – A teapot is the centerpiece on a tiered stand, often accompanied by a milk jug, sugar bowl, and a selection of pastries.
- **Japanese Tea Ceremony (Chanoyu)** – The “kyūsu” teapot is handled with precise, ceremonial movements, reflecting the aesthetic of “wabi‑sabi” (beauty in simplicity and imperfection).
- **Chinese Gongfu Cha** – The Yixing teapot is prized for its ability to “season” over repeated uses, developing a subtle flavor profile that synergizes with the tea leaves.
- **Russian Samovar** – Though technically a larger kettle system, the samovar’s accompanying teapot(s) serve a similar function of brewing individual cups.

### Practical Tips

- **Cleaning** – Rinse with warm water after each use; avoid soap for porous clay teapots, as it can affect flavor. A soft brush can remove residue.
- **Seasoning (Yixing)** – Brew with the same type of tea repeatedly to build up a “tea crust” that enhances future infusions.
- **Storage** – Keep the lid slightly ajar or place a small cloth inside to allow airflow and prevent mildew.
- **Matching Cups** – Pair the teapot with cups of a similar style or material for a cohesive serving experience.
