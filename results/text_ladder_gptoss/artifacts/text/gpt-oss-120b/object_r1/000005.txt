A teapot is a small, usually rounded vessel designed for steeping tea leaves in hot water and then pouring the brewed tea into cups. Its classic design combines both form and function, with several key components:

| Part | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Body (or Vessel)** | The main, bulbous chamber that holds water and tea leaves. It’s often made of ceramic, porcelain, glass, metal (such as cast iron or stainless steel), or even clay. The shape can be a simple squat sphere, a graceful teardrop, or a more decorative silhouette with flutes, spouts, or handles molded into the walls. |
| **Spout** | A narrow, tapered outlet that directs the flow of liquid when the teapot is tilted. It may have a built‑in filter or strainer to keep tea leaves from escaping, and the lip is often beveled to minimize drips. |
| **Lid** | A removable cover that sits snugly on the top of the body, sealing in heat and steam. Lids can be simple flat caps, domed tops, or ornate pieces with decorative knobs or handles. Some lids have a small vent to release excess pressure. |
| **Handle** | An ergonomic projection—often a curved ear‑shaped arm—mounted on the side of the body, allowing the teapot to be lifted and poured safely without burning the hand. Handles can be integrated into the body or attached as a separate piece. |
| **Strainer (optional)** | A built‑in mesh or perforated insert located at the base of the spout or inside the body, catching loose tea leaves as the liquid is poured. Some teapots rely on an external strainer placed in the cup instead. |
| **Infuser (optional)** | A removable compartment, often shaped like a small basket or dome, that holds loose tea while keeping it contained. This makes cleaning easier and lets the user control steeping time. |

### Typical Materials
- **Ceramic/Porcelain:** Retains heat well, offers a smooth, glossy finish, and can be painted or glazed with intricate patterns.
- **Glass:** Transparent, allowing the user to see the color of the steeping tea; typically heat‑resistant borosilicate.
- **Cast Iron:** Heavy, excellent heat retention, often with an enamel interior to prevent rust.
- **Clay (e.g., Yixing):** Porous material that absorbs tea flavors over time, prized by connoisseurs for enhancing the taste of subsequent brews.

### Common Styles
- **English (Traditional):** Sturdy, often with a slightly curved spout and a wide, comfortable handle; usually made of porcelain or stoneware.
- **Japanese (Tetsubin):** Cast‑iron body with a simple, minimalist aesthetic, sometimes featuring a lacquered wooden handle.
- **Chinese (Yixing):** Small, unglazed clay pot that “breathes” and develops a seasoned patina.
- **Modern/Contemporary:** Sleek, often streamlined designs using stainless steel, glass, or silicone; may incorporate ergonomic grips and precision‑engineered spouts.

### Functionality
1. **Heating:** The teapot holds hot water (often just off the boil) and allows the tea leaves to steep, releasing flavor, aroma, and color.
2. **Steeping Control:** Depending on the design, users can let the tea sit inside the pot for a set time or pour the liquid into a separate cup to stop extraction.
3. **Serving:** The spout enables a smooth, controlled pour, minimizing drips and making it easy to serve multiple cups from a single pot.

### Aesthetic Appeal
Beyond its practical role, a teapot often serves as a decorative centerpiece on a kitchen counter or dining table. Its shape, glaze, patterns, and even folk motifs can reflect cultural heritage, personal taste, or seasonal themes.

In summary, a teapot is a versatile, purpose‑built container that blends materials science, ergonomic design, and cultural aesthetics to turn hot water and tea leaves into a comforting, aromatic beverage ready to be shared.
