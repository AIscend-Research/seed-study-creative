The sky has just turned the color of a bruise, a deep indigo bruised by the last streaks of fading amber and copper. A thin veil of mist rolls off the sea, catching the dying light and scattering it in silvery pearls that cling to the jagged cliffs. The shore is a maze of weather‑worn stone—massive basalt boulders smoothed by relentless surf, their edges sharp as teeth, their surfaces slick with salt and sea‑foam. Between the monoliths lie narrow, uneven ledges covered in lichen‑green moss and clumps of wind‑bent grasses that whisper as a gentle breeze stirs them.

The ocean itself is a trembling sheet of slate, its surface broken only by the occasional restless swell that crashes against the rock‑faces in a soft, rolling hiss. Each wave pulls back, leaving behind strands of white foam that cling to the stones like delicate lace before being swallowed again. Farther out, the water darkens to midnight blue, merging with the horizon where the last sliver of sun lingers, tinting the clouds with a faint rose‑gold halo.

Above, the first stars begin to blink into existence, their faint light reflected in the watery pools that have collected in the crevices of the cliffs. A lone gull circles lazily, its silhouette a stark black against the growing dusk. The air is cool, tinged with brine, and carries the faint scent of seaweed and wet stone.

In this twilight moment, the landscape feels both timeless and alive—ancient rocks bearing witness to the endless rhythm of tide and sky, the world hushed between day’s end and night’s arrival.
