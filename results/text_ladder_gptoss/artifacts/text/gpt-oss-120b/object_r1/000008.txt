A teapot is a vessel designed for steeping tea leaves (or tea bags) and then serving the brewed liquid. While designs vary widely across cultures and eras, most teapots share several common features:

**1. Shape and Body**
- **Main Cavity:** The core of the teapot is a rounded, usually bulbous chamber that holds water and tea leaves. It’s often wider at the middle and tapers slightly toward the top, allowing for even circulation of water.
- **Material:** Traditional teapots are made of porous ceramics (like porcelain, stoneware, or earthenware) which retain heat and can slightly enhance flavor. Modern versions may use glass, stainless steel, cast iron, or even silicone. Each material influences heat retention, durability, and aesthetics.

**2. Lid**
- A snug-fitting lid sits atop the cavity, preventing steam from escaping while the tea steeps. Lids often have a small knob or handle for easy removal, and many feature a small hole or vent to release excess pressure and prevent spills.

**3. Spout**
- The spout is a narrow, tapered outlet that directs the brewed tea into a cup without splashing. Its angle and length are crucial: too steep and the tea may dribble; too shallow and it may splash. Some spouts have a “witness” (a small protruding piece) that indicates when the pot is full.

**4. Handle**
- Positioned opposite the spout, the handle provides a comfortable grip for pouring. It’s typically ergonomically curved and insulated from the hot body of the pot, especially in metal teapots where the handle may be made of a different material (e.g., wood or silicone) to prevent burns.

**5. Base**
- The bottom is usually flat for stability, though some decorative or antique teapots feature a raised foot or ornamental base. In cast‑iron teapots, a thick base contributes to excellent heat retention.

**6. Decorative Elements**
- Many teapots are as much art as utility. Common decorative motifs include floral patterns, calligraphy, animal figures, and intricate hand‑painted scenes. In Chinese Yixing teapots, the porous clay can absorb tea oils, subtly enriching flavor over time.

**7. Functional Variations**
- **Infuser Teapots:** Some incorporate a built‑in mesh basket or perforated chamber that holds loose leaves, allowing easy separation of leaves from the brewed tea.
- **Glass Teapots:** Transparent walls let you watch the tea changing color as it steeps.
- **Travel Teapots:** Compact, often insulated, with a spill‑proof lid for on‑the‑go brewing.

**8. Usage Process**
1. **Pre‑heat:** Warm the teapot with hot water to maintain temperature during steeping.
2. **Add Leaves:** Place loose tea or a tea bag inside the pot (or its infuser).
3. **Pour Water:** Add water at the appropriate temperature for the tea type (e.g., 80 °C for green tea, 95 °C for black tea).
4. **Steep:** Let the tea infuse for the recommended time, usually 2–5 minutes.
5. **Serve:** Lift the lid, pour the tea through the spout into cups, and enjoy.

In essence, a teapot combines functional engineering—a well‑balanced body, lid, spout, and handle—with aesthetic expression, making it both a practical kitchen tool and a cultural symbol of hospitality.
